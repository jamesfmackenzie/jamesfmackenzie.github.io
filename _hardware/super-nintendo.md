The SNES was Nintendo's 16-bit follow-up to the NES, and it's one of those consoles where the library alone justifies keeping a unit around. Mine turned out to have more going on inside it than I expected — full story in [my PAL SNES power writeup]({% post_url 2026-09-06-i-just-wanted-to-power-my-pal-snes-in-america %}).

### What it is

Released in Japan in 1990 as the **Super Famicom**, then in North America (1991) and Europe (1992) as the **Super Nintendo Entertainment System**. Same core hardware across regions, but different shells, different cartridge shapes, and region-locking that isn't always as clean in practice as the labeling suggests.

### Why I like it

The library is the whole case: platformers, JRPGs, and Nintendo's own first-party output at a genuine peak. Beyond the games, the hardware has real character of its own — **Mode 7** background scaling and rotation gave the SNES tricks the competition didn't have, and the **SPC700/S-DSP** sound chip still sounds distinct today. The shoulder-button controller shape it introduced is one Nintendo (and everyone else) has never really moved away from.

### Pros

- one of the best 16-bit game libraries ever assembled
- Mode 7 and the SPC700 sound chip gave it a genuinely distinct technical identity
- the shoulder-button controller shape is still influential today
- simple, reliable hardware that's easy to keep running decades later

### Cons

- composite/RF video by default — a clean picture means an RGB or component mod
- region-locking (and the hardware that implements it) can complicate an import console
- cartridge prices for some JRPGs have gotten seriously expensive
