The Nintendo 64 is one of those consoles that is impossible to mistake for anything else.

It did not just push Nintendo into 3D. It did it in a very Nintendo way: cartridge-based, controller-led, multiplayer-heavy, and visually distinctive enough that the machine still has its own immediately recognisable personality.

Despite many happy memories of four-player **F-Zero X** and **GoldenEye** with friends, I was actually very late to the party with the N64 itself. I did not pick one up until a trip to Tokyo in March 2024, where I found an unboxed console and controller at BEEP for **JPY 6,950** (about **$45**) and a second matching controller for **JPY 680** (about **$4.50**).

### What it is

The Nintendo 64 is Nintendo's late-1990s home console and one of the clearest examples of a platform where the hardware experience matters almost as much as the software library.

### Why I keep it

I keep the N64 around because the whole package is so specific:

- a brilliant first-party library
- unmistakable 3D visuals
- legendary couch multiplayer
- a controller design that is awkward, influential, and inseparable from the platform
- some genuine hardware novelty, including the Rumble Pak

It was never just another console from the CD era, and that difference still matters.

![Nintendo 64 controller](/img/hardware/nintendo-64-n64-controller.jpg){: width="680"}

### My take

My take is that the N64 remains one of the strongest arguments for original console hardware.

Back in the day, I was absolutely blown away by N64 graphics. The first time I saw **Super Mario 64**, I was spellbound. Those full 3D worlds felt like a huge leap over the wobblier 3D I associated with the original PlayStation.

The personality of the platform comes from the whole thing working together: cartridge loading, the controller, the multiplayer setup, the Rumble Pak, and the way the video output changes depending on the display chain. It is very hard to reduce that to emulator settings alone.

### Pros

- iconic multiplayer machine
- strong first-party software identity
- no optical drive loading delays
- genuinely exciting early 3D presentation
- memorable hardware innovation like the Rumble Pak
- original hardware still feels especially meaningful

### Cons

- controller design is divisive
- the built-in anti-aliasing blur can be overbearing
- that soft "Vaseline filter" look often ages worse on modern displays
- cartridges kept load times fast but limited storage compared with CD rivals
- the platform missed out on some of the huge-scope JRPG feel that flourished elsewhere
- display setup changes the look of the console a lot
- some of the visual charm can also read as roughness on the wrong setup

### Other useful things to know

- This is a console where CRT presentation, scaling choices, and controller latency all matter more than spec-sheet thinking.
- The N64 can look and feel very different depending on how you connect it.
- The cartridge format is both a strength and a limitation: near-instant loading, but also tighter storage constraints than the CD-based competition.
- For me, it is one of the best reference platforms for thinking about original-hardware play versus modern convenience.
