I unwrapped a PlayStation on Christmas Day 1995, and it felt like the future — proper textured 3D, CD audio, and game worlds far bigger than the cartridge machines it was up against. I've probably logged more hours on it than any console before or since.

### What it is

Sony's first console and one of the defining machines of the 1990s. It pushed home gaming decisively toward 3D and CD-ROM, and it's still common enough that consoles, controllers, memory cards, and cables are easy to find.

### Core specifications

- **CPU:** MIPS R3000A-compatible, 33.8688 MHz
- **Media:** CD-ROM
- **Video:** composite, S-Video, RGB, plus region-dependent RF
- **Audio:** stereo via the AV multi-out
- **Controllers:** digital pad, later Dual Analog and DualShock
- **Storage:** memory cards

Modest by any modern measure, but more than enough to define a generation of 3D console gaming.

### Why I run it on original hardware

The PlayStation is very well emulated now, but a lot of its character lives in the video output — games designed around CRTs, dithering that looks completely different over composite, S-Video, or RGB, and fast 3D that reveals timing and dropped-frame behaviour emulation tends to smooth over. That makes it a genuinely useful reference machine, and I've leaned on it that way for [MiSTer FPGA accuracy testing]({% post_url 2022-12-03-how-accurate-is-mister-fpga-versus-vs-original-hardware %}).

It also has a distinctive accessory ecosystem — the [Namco NeGcon]({% link _hardware/namco-negcon.md %}), the [PlayStation Mouse]({% link _hardware/playstation-mouse.md %}), the GunCon light gun — which is a big part of why the platform still feels specific.
